Context

Roofr could send proposals. It couldn't send anything else.

Roofing jobs generate a lot of paperwork beyond the proposal — sub-contractor agreements, material supplier orders, insurance certificates, change orders, legal releases. Contractors were handling all of it outside Roofr: attach a PDF to an email, follow up manually, hope it gets signed, store it somewhere else.

Every direct competitor — JobNimbus, Leap, JobProgress, SumoQuote — had some form of document signing built in. Roofr had none. The gap wasn't just a missing feature; it was a ceiling on how much of the contractor's workflow Roofr could own.

The design challenge wasn't "build a DocuSign clone." It was: how do you build document signing that feels native to a roofing CRM, integrates with the job data model, and handles legal compliance — without replicating a 20-year-old enterprise UI pattern?

Why this feature is hard to get right

Document signing is deceptively complex. The visible surface — "put a signature field here, send it, get it back" — hides a tangle of design problems underneath: how do you handle multiple signers with different field assignments, PDF rendering fidelity, legal compliance requirements that have to be surfaced in the UI, and a template system that works for both managers setting up standard contracts and reps sending them in the field.

And unlike proposals, there was no existing Roofr pattern to extend. This was a net-new interaction model built from scratch.

Process

Competitive audit first

Before any interaction design, the work was to map what competitors had already figured out — and where they'd gotten it wrong. The audit covered JobNimbus, Leap, JobProgress, SumoQuote, and AccuLynx across both document library and document creation.

Key audit finding: Feature parity wasn't the gap — every competitor had digital signatures. The gap was quality. Most had antiquated UIs, hard-to-find document flows, and no way to send a document directly to a customer without friction. The opportunity was to do it better, not just do it.

Key design decisions

How does the template system relate to the existing Library?
Document templates sit at the same hierarchy as Proposal Templates — not inside the Library. The Library is for storing files; templates are for creating documents to send. Mixing them would have created a confusing mental model for users.
Competitors who reused existing storage UI for templates (JobNimbus) ended up with document types that made no sense in context. Separation of concerns here prevented that.
How is field placement and signer assignment handled?
Field placement is a manager-time action on the template. Reps send the document; they don't place fields. Each field is explicitly assigned to a specific signer (customer vs cosigner) so the signing flow routes correctly without manual coordination.
Mixing template creation and document sending into one flow was the failure mode in every competitor audit. Separating them by role made the rep flow clean and fast.
How is e-signature legal compliance surfaced in the UI?
Two compliance requirements handled in design: intent to sign (the "Sign Now" button is legally sufficient) and consent to do business electronically (consent copy embedded in the signature modal itself — not a separate step). Both surface naturally in the signing flow without interrupting it.
Bolting compliance onto an existing flow as an extra step creates abandonment. Designing it in from the start means it's frictionless and legally sound.
What's the signing order?
V1: Customer first, cosigner if present — mirroring the proposals flow. Redesign: any job contact can be a recipient (Customer, Insurance adjuster, Supplier, Vendor, Crew, Assignee, Owner), with drag-to-reorder signing sequence. The sender appears in the recipient list and can be positioned in the signing order.
V1 mirrored proposals to reduce the learning curve. The redesign's expanded recipient model required a configurable signing order — locking it to "customer first" no longer made sense when the document might be a sub-contractor agreement or insurance claim.
How does the editor structure map to the workflow?
Three-tab structure: Edit (place fields on PDF), Preview (see the document as a recipient would, with contractor fields pre-filled), Send (configure recipients and signing order). A banner in Preview makes the mode explicit: "This is a preview of your order. Switch back to Edit to make changes." The left panel shows a table of contents/page navigator; the right panel surfaces field configuration for the selected element.
Separating Edit/Preview/Send prevents accidental sends and makes the "what am I looking at right now" question unambiguous — a real failure mode when the same PDF is both a template and a document in flight.

Customer feedback as a continuous input

Since GA, I've been actively monitoring and triaging customer feedback — not as a one-time post-beta exercise but as an ongoing part of owning this feature area. I've been meeting with members of the Implementation team to go deeper on what's surfacing: understanding the root problems behind the feedback rather than just the surface requests.

That work has been shaping what gets prioritized next. The high-priority items from the initial beta are still tracked, and the Implementation conversations have added sharper context to what's blocking adoption:

High
Ability to save progress on contractor-filled fields and return to the document — signing sessions can span multiple sittings.
High
Automations specific to document templates — follow-ups, status-triggered actions, reminders after X days unsigned.
High
Separate proposals and signature documents in the job view — users conflating the two in the activity log.
High
Field navigation: "Next" should auto-focus the next fillable field rather than requiring manual click-through.

Strategic Reach

Net-new interaction model, built from scratch

PDF Signer had no existing Roofr pattern to extend. The interaction model — PDF rendering + drag-and-drop field placement + multi-signer assignment + signing workflow + compliance — required designing all of it from first principles, while keeping it consistent with the product's existing mental model where possible (signing order, template hierarchy).

This required close collaboration with engineering throughout the entire development cycle: the rendering pipeline, field positioning and persistence, the signature capture mechanism, and the legal compliance requirements all had design implications that had to be worked through together, not handed off.

Workflow depth, not just surface UX

The research phase — competitive audit, compliance research, closed beta — was designed to surface the workflow questions, not just the UI questions. What's the right role separation between managers and reps? When does compliance need to appear in the flow? What's the right V1 scope vs what's genuinely phase 2? Those decisions shaped what got built, not just how it looked.

Unlocking a significantly larger market

V1 was scoped to homeowner signing — contractors sending documents to customers. The internal use case is a much larger opportunity: sub-contractor agreements, supplier orders, insurance certificates, internal sign-offs. These are documents that exist entirely within the contractor's own operations, sent between team members and trade partners rather than homeowners.

The feedback monitoring and Implementation team conversations have made it clear this is where the ceiling is. The current product works for customer-facing documents — it's the internal workflow that's blocked, and unblocking it is what moves PDF Signer from a useful feature to a core part of how a roofing business runs on Roofr.
