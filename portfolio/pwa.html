Roofr PWA & Mobile Redesign

Role Lead Designer
Scope Proposals, CRM, Inbox, Work Orders, Calendar

Context

Roofr has no native app

Every competitor — Jobber, JobNimbus, CompanyCam — has native iOS and Android apps. Roofr is web-only. That's not temporary; it's a constraint the product has to live inside. Which means the mobile web experience isn't a fallback. For field users, it's the product.

Sales reps update jobs from driveways between appointments. Crew leads reference work orders on-site. Homeowners sign proposals on their phones. These are core workflows — not edge cases — and the product was failing them.

The problem wasn't responsiveness. It was interaction models.

The existing mobile experience was technically responsive — layouts reflow, columns stack — but the interaction model was still desktop-native. Fixed sidebars that became full-width overlays. Top toolbars that required two-thumb reach. Inline editing on tiny tap targets. The layouts adapted; the mental model didn't.

The design question: For every interaction pattern in the product, what is its mobile-native equivalent? Not a scaled-down desktop — a pattern designed for touch, one-handed use, and the physical context of a job site.

Process

Audit → Pattern system → Component build

The work began with a full interaction audit: every screen, every pattern, catalogued against its touch failure mode. Where did the pattern rely on hover states? Precise cursor targeting? Fixed-position UI that assumed a large viewport? Those were the redesign candidates.

Three mobile-native patterns emerged as the building blocks of the redesign:

  • FAB (Floating Action Button) — replaces top toolbars and formatting bars. Single tap to expand, context-aware actions.
  • Pull card — replaces persistent sidebars. Triggered by tap or swipe-up gesture; lives at the bottom of the screen within thumb reach.
  • Bottom sheet — replaces inline editing. Focused, dismissable panel that doesn't interrupt the full page view.

Every proposal, CRM, inbox, work order, calendar, and automations interaction was redesigned using these three patterns as primitives. Not designed once — designed consistently, with the same behavioral rules applied across the platform.

Annotation-driven dev handoff

Mobile behavior is harder to spec than desktop: gestures, edge cases, conditional states. Every meaningful interaction in the Figma file includes a structured behavior annotation — trigger, response, edge case, exception. Gesture specs are explicit. Error states are designed, not assumed.

This approach reduced back-and-forth during development significantly and became the standard for handoff across the proposals team.

Mobile proposals sent grew from 1,935 in March 2024 to 8,301 in March 2026 — every one of those sent from a mobile web session, with no native app. The PWA reached 18% of all proposals sent within two months of launch, going from 2,280 in February to 10,292 in March 2026.

The component patterns established here — pull cards, FABs, bottom sheets — are now the standard mobile primitives for the proposals team. The change orders mobile work (PRO-14724) is being built directly on top of them.

Strategic Reach

This is Roofr's entire mobile strategy

Without native apps, improving the mobile web experience isn't a UX initiative — it's the product strategy for field users. Sales reps, crew leads, and homeowners interact with Roofr through a phone browser. The quality of that experience directly affects whether they use the product at all.

Decisions made in this project — which patterns are mobile-native, how handoffs are documented, how PWA capabilities are structured — aren't scoped to one feature. They set the standard for how every future mobile surface gets designed.

PM-level thinking applied

The scope prioritization here was a product decision, not just a design one: which product areas needed mobile-native redesigns first, versus which could ship with responsive-scaling as a temporary fix? That required understanding usage data, competitive pressure (competitors have native apps), and engineering capacity constraints.
