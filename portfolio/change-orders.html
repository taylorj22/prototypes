Context

Scope changes had nowhere to live

Every roofing job changes after the contract is signed. The scope shifts, materials get swapped, the customer adds a skylight. Roofr had no dedicated way to handle post-sale scope changes — so contractors built their own workarounds.

Create a second proposal
No link to the original contract. No approval trail. No way to distinguish the final agreed scope from what was first sent. Tracking becomes a nightmare.
Use PDF Signer or external signing tools
The change gets documented, but it lives outside Roofr — disconnected from the job, the invoice, and the financial picture. Downstream value never materialises.
Track changes manually (or not at all)
No structure, no visibility, no approval record. Slow to follow up, impossible to audit, and nothing flows to invoicing or job costing.

This breaks everything downstream

Job costing becomes inaccurate
Margin calculations don't reflect the real scope. Profitability reporting is wrong.
Reporting is incomplete
Revenue attributed to change orders doesn't appear in the performance dashboard.
Invoicing doesn't reflect reality
The invoice goes out for the original scope. The extra work either doesn't get billed or gets billed manually outside the system.
Material orders get disconnected
Updated scope doesn't flow to material orders. Field crews work from stale information.

The design question: What if change orders lived inside the proposal flow? Keep scope changes tied to the original contract. Keep data connected. Eliminate the duplicate work — and make sure every approved change syncs downstream automatically.

Engineering jams — earlier alignment, fewer surprises

Alongside the design work, I introduced a new format with the Proposals engineering team: small working sessions early in the design process, before specs were locked. The goal was to surface the "why" behind design decisions before handoff, and to use engineering's technical perspective to stress-test patterns before they became hard to change. Edge cases and data model questions that would otherwise surface during QA got resolved in these sessions instead.

Key design decisions

What is the relationship between a change order and the original proposal?
A change order is tied to a specific won proposal — not created as a standalone document. This preserves contract history and makes the change legible in context of the original agreement.
Deferred decision would have created a data model inconsistency that's hard to untangle retroactively.
When a change order is approved, how does it affect the invoice?
Two options: append the change order amount to an existing invoice, or generate a separate invoice. Both are supported. The contractor chooses at the time of approval — discovered via user research that both workflows are common.
Not deferring this to "phase 2" — half-built invoicing integration would create user confusion about where the money goes.
When does a change order update job costing?
On approval, not on creation. An unapproved change order represents potential scope — not committed revenue. Job costing reflects committed scope only.
Getting this wrong in the data model means reporting shows incorrect job margins until the inconsistency is fixed.
How does the change order connect to material orders and work orders?
Short-term: manual connection (contractor creates MO/WO separately). Long-term: approved change orders auto-generate MO and WO line items. The data model is designed for the long-term even if the feature ships at short-term scope.
Designing only for phase 1 scope would require a data migration when the automation feature ships.

Strategic Reach

Designing for the system, not the feature

The most important design work on change orders wasn't the UI — it was the upstream decisions: what is the data model, how do the integrations work, what gets deferred and what can't be. These are product architecture decisions made before a screen was designed.

Deferring the invoice integration, the job costing logic, or the data model relationship to "phase 2" would have shipped a feature that looked complete but created debt in every adjacent system. The design process forced these decisions to be made explicitly rather than implicitly.

Cross-team coordination

Six cross-product dependencies meant six conversations across teams. The change orders design touched the invoices team, the job costing implementation, the performance dashboard, the material orders data model, and the work orders flow. Coordinating those dependencies — surfacing where decisions in one area constrained choices in another — was a significant part of the work.

IRE — validating with real contractors before GA

Before Change Orders reached general availability, I prepared a specific set of prototypes for Nic to share at IRE (International Roofing Expo). This was product advocacy outside normal channels: putting the design in front of real contractors at an industry event to get reactions before the feature shipped. The feedback from those conversations informed prioritization decisions that carried into development.

Mobile built on the PWA foundation

The 77 mobile components established in the PWA redesign paid forward directly to Change Orders. Mobile Change Orders (PRO-14724) was built on top of the existing pull cards, FABs, and bottom sheet patterns — and was completed in less than half the originally estimated timeframe as a result.

Foundation for automation

The long-term vision — change orders that auto-generate work orders and material orders, update job costing in real time, trigger automations, and flow into QuickBooks — is only achievable because the data model was designed for it from the start. The phase 1 scope is conservative. The architecture is not.
